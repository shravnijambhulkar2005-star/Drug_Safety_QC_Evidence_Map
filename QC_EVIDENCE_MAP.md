# 1. Purpose of This Document

This document explains how **laboratory quality control (QC)** can support pharmaceutical quality investigations and drug-safety evidence review.

A safety-related observation may sometimes be connected with information about:

- Product quality.
- Manufacturing batches/lots.
- Sample testing.
- Laboratory investigations.
- Deviations.
- Out-of-specification results.
- Product complaints.

Laboratory QC evidence can provide important supporting information during an investigation.

However, an important scientific principle must always be followed:

```text
QC/Laboratory Finding
≠
Automatic Proof That a Product Caused an Adverse Event
```

Similarly:

```text
Quality Defect
≠
Automatic Proof of Patient-Level Causality
```

QC evidence must be reviewed together with other available safety, scientific, manufacturing, and product information.

# 2. Role of QC in the Drug-Safety Evidence System

A drug-safety investigation may begin with a safety observation or reported event.

In some situations, the investigation may require a review of the relevant product or batch.

The general relationship can be represented as:

```text
Safety Observation
        ↓
Evidence Report
        ↓
Product Identification
        ↓
Batch/Lot Traceability
        ↓
Quality Complaint Review
        ↓
Sample and QC Investigation
        ↓
Laboratory Evidence
        ↓
Evidence Validation
        ↓
Scientific Assessment
        ↓
Human Review
        ↓
Documentation and Follow-up
```

The purpose of QC evidence is to answer questions such as:

- Was the correct product or batch identified?
- Was there a reported or confirmed quality problem?
- Was the laboratory sample properly identified?
- Was the sample maintained in an appropriate condition?
- Was the instrument suitable for testing?
- Were appropriate controls and reference standards used?
- Were there deviations during testing?
- Is the laboratory result reliable and traceable?
- Does the QC information support, contradict, or fail to clarify the safety investigation?

QC information should support evidence review rather than automatically generate a clinical conclusion.

# 3. QA and QC: Basic Difference

## Quality Assurance (QA)

Quality Assurance is the broader system used to help ensure that processes are planned, controlled, documented, and performed according to applicable quality requirements.

QA is mainly concerned with building and maintaining systems that help prevent quality problems.

Examples include:

- Standard operating procedures.
- Training.
- Documentation systems.
- Change control.
- Audits.
- Deviation management.
- Quality management systems.

## Quality Control (QC)

Quality Control is more directly concerned with checking and testing materials, samples, and products.

Examples include:

- Laboratory testing.
- Sample analysis.
- Instrument checks.
- Calibration.
- Reference standards.
- Controls.
- Result review.

A simple comparison is:

```text
QA
= Preventive Quality System

QC
= Testing and Checking Activities
```

Both are important for reliable pharmaceutical evidence.

```text
Product/Safety Concern Identified
        ↓
Product and Batch/Lot Identified
        ↓
Sample Received
        ↓
Sample Identification Verified
        ↓
Sample Integrity Checked
        ↓
Testing Method Confirmed
        ↓
Instrument Calibration/Status Checked
        ↓
Reference Standard and Controls Checked
        ↓
Laboratory Test Performed
        ↓
Replicate/Consistency Review
        ↓
Results Recorded
        ↓
Data Integrity and Audit Trail Review
        ↓
Result Compared With Applicable Approved Criteria
        ↓
OOS/OOT/Deviation Review if Applicable
        ↓

        ↓

        ↓
Human Scientific Review
        ↓
Documentation and Follow-up
```

This workflow is a conceptual model for evidence organization.

It is not a substitute for applicable GMP procedures, validated methods, approved specifications, or regulatory requirements.

# 5. QC Evidence Map by Stage

## Stage 1 — Product or Safety Concern Identified

### Input

A safety observation, adverse-event report, drug-quality complaint, or other evidence requiring review.

### QC Activity

Determine whether the investigation requires product-quality or laboratory information.

### Output

Decision regarding whether relevant QC evidence should be collected.

### Evidence Required

- Product identification.
- Product name.
- Available batch/lot information.
- Nature of the concern.
- Relevant dates.

### Possible Failure

- Incorrect product identified.
- Batch information missing.
- Safety event incorrectly linked to a product.

### Validation Requirement

Confirm the available product identifiers and document missing information.

---

## Stage 2 — Batch/Lot Traceability

### Input

Product and available batch/lot information.

### QC Activity

Trace the relevant batch/lot through available records.

### Output

Linked batch/lot information.

### Evidence Required

- Batch/lot number.
- Manufacturing records where relevant.
- QC records where relevant.
- Distribution or complaint information where applicable.

### Possible Failure

- Missing batch number.
- Incorrect batch linkage.
- Incomplete records.
- Loss of traceability.

### Validation Requirement

Verify identifiers against available records.

---

## Stage 3 — Sample Identification

### Input

Laboratory sample received for investigation or testing.

### QC Activity

Assign or confirm a unique sample identifier.

### Output

Traceable laboratory sample.

### Evidence Required

- Sample ID.
- Product name.
- Batch/lot number.
- Date received.
- Source of sample.
- Test request.

### Possible Failure

- Sample mix-up.
- Incorrect labeling.
- Missing sample information.

### Validation Requirement

Verify the sample identity before testing.

---

## Stage 4 — Sample Integrity

### Input

Identified laboratory sample.

### QC Activity

Check whether the sample appears suitable for testing.

### Considerations

- Container condition.
- Label condition.
- Storage history where available.
- Possible contamination.
- Possible damage.
- Temperature conditions where relevant.
- Time between collection and testing where relevant.

### Output

Sample accepted for testing, rejected, or flagged for review.

### Possible Failure

- Degraded sample.
- Contamination.
- Incorrect storage.
- Unknown sample history.

### Validation Requirement

Document the sample condition and any concerns before testing.

---

## Stage 5 — Test Method Confirmation

### Input

Accepted sample and requested analysis.

### QC Activity

Confirm that the appropriate approved or validated analytical method is available.

### Output

Confirmed testing method.

### Evidence Required

- Method identification.
- Current approved version.
- Test procedure.
- Applicable specification or acceptance criteria.

### Possible Failure

- Incorrect method used.
- Obsolete method version.
- Unapproved modification.

### Validation Requirement

Verify the method before testing begins.

---

## Stage 6 — Instrument Calibration and Status

### Input

Selected analytical method.

### QC Activity

Confirm that the required instrument is in an appropriate status for use.

### Evidence Required

- Calibration status.
- Maintenance status.
- Instrument performance checks.
- Relevant logbooks or records.

### Output

Instrument accepted for use or flagged for investigation.

### Possible Failure

- Expired calibration.
- Instrument malfunction.
- Incomplete maintenance.
- Failed performance checks.

### Validation Requirement

Review instrument status before accepting results.

---

## Stage 7 — Reference Standards and Controls

### Input

Testing method and available laboratory materials.

### QC Activity

Use appropriate reference standards and controls as required by the method.

### Purpose

Reference standards and controls help demonstrate whether the analytical system is performing as expected.

### Output

Supporting evidence for analytical-system suitability.

### Possible Failure

- Incorrect standard used.
- Expired or unsuitable standard.
- Control failure.
- Improper preparation.

### Validation Requirement

Verify identity, status, preparation, and suitability according to the applicable procedure.

---

## Stage 8 — Laboratory Testing

### Input

Accepted sample, approved method, suitable instrument, and appropriate controls.

### QC Activity

Perform the laboratory test according to the applicable procedure.

### Output

Raw and processed laboratory data.

### Evidence Required

- Raw data.
- Instrument output.
- Calculations.
- Observations.
- Test records.

### Possible Failure

- Procedural error.
- Transcription error.
- Equipment issue.
- Sample mix-up.
- Calculation error.

### Validation Requirement

Testing records should be reviewed for completeness and traceability.

---

## Stage 9 — Replicates and Consistency Review

### Input

Initial laboratory measurements.

### QC Activity

Review replicate measurements or repeated measurements where required by the method.

### Purpose

Replicates can help evaluate consistency and analytical variability.

### Output

Consistency information.

### Possible Failure

- Unexpected variability.
- Inconsistent measurements.
- Unexplained analytical differences.

### Validation Requirement

Any unusual variability should be assessed according to the applicable procedure.

# 6. Measurement Uncertainty

Measurement uncertainty recognizes that a laboratory measurement is not always perfectly exact.

A result may have a range of uncertainty depending on factors such as:

- Instrument performance.
- Method variability.
- Sample preparation.
- Reference materials.
- Environmental conditions.
- Analyst-related factors.

Measurement uncertainty should be considered when scientifically interpreting laboratory results.

However, this project does not calculate or invent uncertainty limits.

The applicable validated method or laboratory procedure should define how measurement uncertainty is evaluated.

# 7. Result Recording and Data Integrity

After testing, results must be recorded in a clear and traceable manner.

Important information may include:

- Sample ID.
- Product name.
- Batch/lot number.
- Test method.
- Instrument ID.
- Date of testing.
- Analyst information where applicable.
- Raw data.
- Calculations.
- Final result.
- Deviations.
- Review status.

The central principle is:

```text
Reliable QC Evidence
Requires
Reliable and Traceable Data
```

Poor data integrity may include:

- Missing records.
- Unexplained changes.
- Incorrect transcription.
- Duplicate records.
- Missing raw data.
- Unclear corrections.
- Loss of traceability.

# 8. Audit Trails

An audit trail is a record that helps show important actions, changes, or events related to electronic data.

Depending on the system, an audit trail may help answer:

- Who made a change?
- What was changed?
- When was it changed?
- Why was the change made, where applicable?

Audit trails can support:

- Traceability.
- Data integrity.
- Investigation.
- Review of unexpected changes.

The presence of an audit trail does not automatically guarantee that the data is scientifically correct, but it can help investigate the history of a record.

# 9. Out-of-Specification (OOS) Results

An OOS result is generally a result that does not meet the applicable approved specification or acceptance criteria.

A simplified OOS evidence pathway is:

```text
Test Performed
        ↓
Result Obtained
        ↓
Compare With Applicable Approved Specification
        ↓
Result Outside Specification
        ↓
OOS Investigation
        ↓
Check Data and Calculations
        ↓
Check Sample and Method
        ↓
Check Instrument and Procedure
        ↓
Document Investigation
        ↓
Quality Review
```

An OOS result should be investigated according to the applicable quality system.

Important principle:

```text
OOS Result
≠
Automatic Proof of Manufacturing Failure
```

A proper investigation is required.

This project does not invent any OOS acceptance limits.

The applicable specification, validated method, or approved quality requirement must provide the relevant criteria.

---

# 10. Out-of-Trend (OOT) Results

An OOT result is a result that appears unusual when compared with previous, historical, or expected data patterns.

An OOT result may require investigation even if it remains within an established specification.

A simplified OOT process is:

```text
Current Result
        ↓
Compare With Relevant Historical/Expected Trend
        ↓
Unusual Pattern Identified
        ↓
Review Data and Method
        ↓
Review Possible Analytical or Process Factors
        ↓
Document Assessment
        ↓
Human Quality Review
```

Important principle:

```text
OOT Result
≠
Automatic Product Failure
```

Further investigation may be needed.

---

# 11. Deviations

A deviation is a departure from an approved procedure, instruction, or expected process.

Examples may include:

- Incorrect procedure followed.
- Instrument issue.
- Sample-handling problem.
- Environmental condition outside the expected range.
- Documentation error.
- Delay in an activity.
- Use of an incorrect material.

A deviation should be:

```text
Identified
        ↓
Documented
        ↓
Investigated
        ↓
Assessed for Impact
        ↓
Reviewed
        ↓
Linked to Relevant Evidence
```

The effect of a deviation should not be assumed without proper assessment.

---

# 12. Equipment Maintenance

Laboratory equipment requires appropriate maintenance to support reliable testing.

Relevant evidence may include:

- Maintenance schedules.
- Maintenance records.
- Calibration records.
- Instrument qualification status.
- Performance checks.
- Repair history where applicable.

An equipment problem may be important when investigating an unusual laboratory result.

However:

```text
Instrument Problem Identified
≠
Automatic Proof That Every Result Is Invalid
```

The impact should be assessed using appropriate procedures and evidence.

# 13. Result Review and Approval

Before QC information is used as supporting evidence, results should undergo appropriate review.

A simplified review pathway is:

```text
Analyst Completes Test
        ↓
Raw Data Reviewed
        ↓
Calculations Checked
        ↓
Method Compliance Checked
        ↓
Instrument Status Checked
        ↓
Deviations Reviewed
        ↓
Result Evaluated
        ↓
Appropriate Approval
        ↓
Result Available as QC Evidence
```

The responsible role may vary according to the laboratory and quality system.

Potential reviewers may include:

- Analyst.
- Senior analyst.
- QC reviewer.
- Laboratory supervisor.
- QA personnel.

# 14. Batch/Lot Traceability

Batch/lot traceability is an important link between pharmaceutical quality information and safety evidence.

A proposed traceability model is:

```text
Drug/Product
        ↓
Product Identifier
        ↓
Batch/Lot Number
        ↓
Manufacturing Information
        ↓
QC Testing Records
        ↓
Distribution Information
        ↓
Quality Complaints
        ↓
Safety Evidence
        ↓
Investigation Record
```

This allows future investigators to ask whether multiple observations are connected to:

- The same batch.
- Different batches.
- A specific manufacturing period.
- A specific product version.

However, repeated events associated with one batch may represent a possible pattern requiring investigation and do not independently prove causality.

For this project, laboratory data should aim to be:

- Attributable.
- Legible/readable.
- Recorded appropriately.
- Accurate.
- Complete.
- Consistent.
- Enduring.
- Available when needed.

Additional principles may include ensuring that data remains:

- Complete.
- Consistent.
- Enduring.
- Available.

A simplified data-integrity workflow is:

```text
Data Generated
        ↓
Original Record Preserved
        ↓
Changes Traceable
        ↓
Review Performed
        ↓
Data Linked to Sample/Batch
        ↓
Evidence Provenance Preserved
```

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Evidence ID | Unique identifier for the evidence |
| Product Identifier | Identifies the relevant product |
| Batch/Lot Number | Supports traceability |
| Sample ID | Identifies the tested sample |
| QC Test Type | Identifies the laboratory test |
| Method ID | Identifies the testing method |
| Instrument ID | Identifies the equipment used |
| Calibration Status | Records instrument suitability |
| Reference Standard/Control | Records analytical comparison material |
| Test Date | Records when testing occurred |
| Result | Records the laboratory outcome |
| Replicate Information | Supports consistency review |
| Measurement Uncertainty | Records applicable uncertainty information |
| OOS/OOT Status | Identifies unusual results where applicable |
| Deviation ID | Links relevant deviations |
| Supporting Documentation | Provides traceability |
| Reviewer | Records review responsibility where applicable |
| Validation Status | Shows evidence-check status |
| Limitations | Records known limitations |
| Human Review Flag | Indicates need for further review |

These fields are intended for evidence organization and should not be treated as a complete regulatory laboratory information-management system.

# 17. Hypothetical QC Workflow Example

## Scenario

A fictional product called **Drug A Tablets** is associated with several reported safety observations.

A quality investigation is initiated to determine whether relevant product or laboratory information should be reviewed.

### Step 1 — Product Identification

```text
Product: Drug A Tablets
Batch/Lot: LOT-EXAMPLE-001
```

The available product and batch information is recorded.

### Step 2 — Sample Receipt

A sample associated with the investigation is received by the laboratory.

A unique sample ID is assigned:

```text
Sample ID: SAMPLE-EXAMPLE-001
```

### Step 3 — Sample Integrity Check

The laboratory checks:

- Label condition.
- Container condition.
- Available storage information.
- Sample identification.

Any concern is documented before testing.

### Step 4 — Method Confirmation

The laboratory confirms that an appropriate approved or validated method is available.

The method and version are recorded.

### Step 5 — Instrument Check

The required analytical instrument is checked for:

- Appropriate calibration status.
- Required maintenance.
- Suitable performance status.

### Step 6 — Reference Standard and Control Check

Required reference standards and controls are verified according to the applicable procedure.

### Step 7 — Testing

The sample is tested using the applicable method.

Raw data and observations are recorded.

### Step 8 — Result Review

The result is reviewed together with:

- Raw data.
- Calculations.
- Replicate information where applicable.
- Instrument status.
- Deviations.
- Applicable approved specification or validated method requirements.

### Step 9 — Unusual Result Handling

If the result is unusual:

```text
Unusual Result
        ↓
Do Not Immediately Assume Cause
        ↓
Check Data
        ↓
Check Calculations
        ↓
Check Sample
        ↓
Check Method
        ↓
Check Instrument
        ↓
Review Deviations
        ↓
Document Investigation
        ↓
Human Quality Review
```

### Step 10 — Link to Safety Investigation

The QC finding is added as structured supporting evidence.

Possible statuses may include:

- Supports further investigation.
- Does not support the proposed quality concern.
- Incomplete evidence.
- Conflicting evidence.
- Requires human review.

Important final principle:

```text
QC Finding Supports Evidence Review

It Does Not Independently Establish
Patient-Level Drug Causality
```

# 18. QC Failure Possibilities and Controls

| QC Stage | Possible Failure | Example Control/Validation |
|---|---|---|
| Product Identification | Wrong product linked | Verify product identifiers |
| Batch Traceability | Incorrect batch number | Cross-check available records |
| Sample Receipt | Sample mix-up | Unique sample ID |
| Sample Integrity | Damaged/degraded sample | Condition and storage review |
| Method Selection | Incorrect/obsolete method | Method version verification |
| Instrument Status | Calibration or maintenance issue | Review instrument records |
| Reference Standard | Incorrect/unsuitable standard | Identity and suitability check |
| Testing | Procedural or analytical error | Procedure and raw-data review |
| Replicates | Unexplained variability | Consistency assessment |
| Result Recording | Transcription error | Independent review |
| Data Integrity | Unexplained changes | Audit trail and record review |
| OOS/OOT | Incorrect interpretation | Formal investigation process |
| Deviation | Impact not assessed | Document and assess deviation |
| Final Review | Unsupported approval | Appropriate reviewer approval |

This document is a conceptual and educational evidence map.

It does not:

- Replace GMP requirements.

- Define regulatory acceptance limits.

The appropriate validated method, approved specification, laboratory procedure, and regulatory requirements must be followed in real investigations.

Correct Identification

+
Suitable Instrument Status
+
Reference Standards and Controls
+
Reliable Data
+

+
Result Review

A laboratory result should not be interpreted in isolation.

QC information should be connected with:

- Safety reports.
- Product information.
- Batch/lot traceability.
- Quality complaints.
- Supporting documentation.
- Evidence limitations.

The central principle of this evidence map is:

```text
QC Evidence
+
Safety Evidence
+
Validation
+
Documentation
+
Human Scientific Review
```

However:

```text
QC Evidence
≠
Automatic Proof of Causality
```
